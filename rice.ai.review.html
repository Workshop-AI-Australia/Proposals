AI Review and Recommendations

From working to lasting

Rice Group has built more working AI than most businesses its size: tools in daily use, real workflows running, a team that's already started. The opportunity now isn't more of that, it's structure. Most of what's been built works because one or two people hold it up, which makes it powerful but dependent, and it stops at the edge of the people who made it. The value here is in giving that work a foundation that lets it hold, run without a single person behind it, and spread across the team.

This review sets out each piece of that work, where it sits, and how to build on it, then the foundation to start with and the order to take the rest. That foundation also unlocks the more interesting question underneath all of it: not how to do the current work more efficiently, but how the data and systems you'd be building could let the business compete on something its rivals can't match. The pieces, the sequence, and that longer game all follow.

01 · What runs across all of itFive patterns, one story.

A few patterns show up in almost every piece, and they're the real reason the foundation matters. Step through them. Most of what follows is the same fix wearing different clothes: take something that needs a particular person, and make it run without one.

Pattern 01 of 05

Built to work once, not to keep working.

Most of the fragility traces to one thing: these systems were built to run on the day they were made, by the person who made them, with nothing put around them to keep them running.

The index works until a document is added. The reconciliation check worked until it hit an edge case. The approval app worked until it began to drift. None was designed to sustain itself, so each one quietly decays the moment conditions change. They are prototypes doing the job of infrastructure, and nothing was built for the gap between the two.

Pattern 02 of 05

The capability sits with one or two people.

Knowing how any of this works, and being able to fix it when it breaks, rests with one or two people. This is the real business risk, more than any technical one.

The agent is single-user. The project pattern is in your head. The routing logic is in Cassie's. The reconciliation logic was hard to recover the moment it changed hands. The systems the business is coming to rely on can only be run or repaired by the people who built them, and those people have no spare time. The self-maintaining index is the clearest fix: it turns a one-person job into one that runs on its own.

Pattern 03 of 05

Nothing learns from its mistakes.

Systems across the stack make errors and have no way to capture them. So nothing improves. It just gets patched, and the same problems come back.

The reconciliation check kept no error history. The approval app can't be shown what it got wrong. Routing decisions are made and then lost. The fix repeats too: a logged record of what each system did and what a person corrected. That turns a black box into something that gets better, and it finally makes the error rate visible.

Pattern 04 of 05

No shared standard, so good work doesn't spread.

Work out a good way to do something and it stays yours. The value one person creates doesn't travel, so the business keeps getting the output of one person instead of thirty.

The index pattern is a strong idea living on one machine. A Cowork process gets set up, then drifts, because there's no agreed way to use it. This isn't about rules for their own sake. A shared standard that people actually follow is what lets good work compound across the team.

Pattern 05 of 05

Control on platforms you don't own.

A few core workflows run on tools where the vendor holds the controls. You're alert to this and already moving on it.

The approval app is locked inside a platform you can't see into or change. The agent leans on a home machine and an email account that's been shut off once already. Keep it as a lens when choosing where the next pieces live: prefer ground you can own and inspect.

The control scales with the work. Open work stays open. Shared work gets light rails. High-stakes, repeatable work gets full ones. 04 · The end stateOne joined-up system.

The centralised project system you started building with Fable shows the direction this can go: a single source and system for the team, one framework that can be monitored, measured and maintained. The way to get there is to build the individual pieces first, each one designed to fit the end state from the start. Each would have the same owned store, the same logged-record pattern, and the same shared interface across all of them.

Build it this way and each piece delivers on its own, each teaches you what the next should be, and because every piece is built against the same end state, you arrive at one joined-up system rather than a set of tools bolted together. The foundation is the first of these pieces. The rest sit on it. The order they go in is next.

05 · Where to startThe foundation, then a piece at a time.

The foundation comes first, everything else stands on it: a shared, agreed understanding of how AI is used, what's off-limits, what data never goes near it, and how the tools are kept. Past that the order isn't fixed, it firms up as we go, but the work breaks down roughly like this.

Phase 01Foundation & quick wins
  • The governance program: a facilitated four-week build of the company-wide guide, training, and a change process, co-created so it's actually followed.
  • The self-maintaining project index, built into the project-setup standard so every project inherits it. High reach, used daily, a strong early win.
Phase 02The reconciliation checks
  • The daily check, rebuilt as a bounded check with its own logged history, putting the financial safety net back in place.
  • The month-end review, built as an extension of that check rather than a separate system.
Phase 03The invoice pipeline
  • Invoice routing, the agent that places invoices and learns from Cassie's corrections.
  • The approval app, migrated off Lovable onto owned infrastructure with a corrections log.
  • The two joined into one flow, with the clean-pass measure across the whole pipeline.
Phase 04Watch & shift
  • Bob's jobs move to native tooling as the cloud-scheduling and messaging pieces mature.
  • The bigger build assembles from these components over time, rather than as one large release.

This is a direction, not a timetable. Each piece earns its place as we learn more, and the order flexes around what proves most pressing once we're in. Each piece is built toward the same end state, so it's useful on its own the day it ships and part of the whole the day after.

06 · The longer gameA second horizon, once the basics are solid.

Everything above makes the current work better. Faster routing, checks that remember, an index that maintains itself, a team that all works the same way. It's the right place to start, and most businesses never even get this far. But it's worth being clear about what it is: it's playing the same game better. Doing what you already do, more efficiently and more reliably.

There's a second horizon, and you've probably already started circling it. The same tools and the same data could be used not to do the current work faster, but to do something your competitors can't. That's a different move. Less "AI helps us run the business" and more "what we've quietly accumulated becomes something nobody else has." Two places it shows up:

The data you're already sitting on
  • Years of invoices, job costs, creditor behaviour and project financials, across multiple entities. Right now it's admin exhaust, used once and filed.
  • Organised, it could make you faster and sharper than competitors at the things that win work: pricing a job, reading its risk, knowing which clients and jobs are worth it. That's not efficiency, it's an advantage that compounds and that someone starting fresh can't copy, because they don't have the history.
The agricultural arm
  • The cleanest example of the same idea in miniature. The weigh station emails weight data every morning and it goes nowhere, a stream of operational data most operations simply don't have organised, sitting unused.
  • The question isn't "how do we file this faster," it's "what decision could this data make better that competitors are making on gut feel": when to sell, what to buy, how the herd's actually tracking. Small, but it's the whole principle in one paddock.

We're not proposing either of these now. The foundation comes first, and these only become real once it's down. But they're worth holding in mind as the work progresses, because the more interesting question, once the basics are solid, isn't how to do the current game more efficiently. It's whether the data and the coordination you'd be building could quietly change what game you're playing. That's a conversation for down the track, and one you're well placed to have.
